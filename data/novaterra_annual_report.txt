Novaterra Energy AG - Annual Report 2025 (Excerpt)

Letter to Shareholders

Novaterra Energy AG is a utility headquartered in Linz, Austria, that generates and distributes electricity to roughly 1.8 million households in Central Europe. Founded in 1998 as a regional grid operator, it has spent the last decade turning itself into a renewable-first power producer.

2025 was a year of transition. The company completed the sale of its last two coal-fired plants in March and redirected the proceeds into wind and battery storage. Management believes this makes the group one of the few mid-sized European utilities with no remaining exposure to coal.

Financial Performance

The company's revenue rose to EUR 4.2 billion in 2025, an increase of 9 percent over the prior year. Growth was driven mainly by higher output from its onshore wind portfolio and by new long-term supply contracts with industrial customers. EBITDA reached EUR 910 million, while net income came in at EUR 312 million. The board proposes a dividend of EUR 1.40 per share, up from EUR 1.25.

Its balance sheet remained solid. Net debt fell to EUR 2.1 billion, and the leverage ratio (net debt to EBITDA) improved from 2.8x to 2.3x. The company intends to keep leverage below 3.0x throughout the current investment cycle.

Operations

The flagship asset is the Kessel Ridge wind farm in Lower Austria. It added 140 megawatts of capacity in 2025 and is now the largest onshore wind site in the group, with a total of 410 megawatts. Availability at the site averaged 97.2 percent during the year.

A second focus area is grid-scale storage. In October the group commissioned a 200 megawatt-hour battery facility next to the Kessel Ridge site. The facility allows the company to store surplus wind power at night and sell it during evening demand peaks, when prices are highest.

Outlook

For 2026, management expects revenue between EUR 4.4 and 4.6 billion. Capital expenditure is planned at EUR 1.1 billion, of which about 70 percent will go into new wind capacity and the remainder into grid upgrades. The main risks it identifies are lower wholesale power prices, delays in permitting, and rising turbine costs.
